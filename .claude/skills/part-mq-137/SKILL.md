# MQ-137 ammonia gas sensor module (MQ-137)

## Overview
The **MQ-137** (Winsen / Hanwei) is a heated tin-dioxide (SnO2) gas sensor for **ammonia (NH3)**. It also responds to organic amines such as trimethylamine. Its resistance **Rs drops** as NH3 rises. Hobbyists buy it on a **4-pin module** (common blue board) that adds a load resistor, an **LM393 comparator**, a sensitivity trimmer, a power LED and a DO LED.
- Detection range: **5–500 ppm NH3** (datasheet). The module shows **more or less ammonia than before**. It is **not a calibrated ppm meter**
- Sensitivity: Rs(in air) / Rs(50 ppm NH3) ≥ 2. Concentration slope R200ppm/R50ppm ≤ 0.6
- Heater: **5.0 V ±0.1 V**, 29 Ω ±3 Ω, ≤ 900 mW (about **170 mA at 5 V**, about 180 mA for the whole module). It gets hot
- Preheat: **over 48 hours** for a new sensor (72 h after 1–6 months of storage, 168 h after more), then a few minutes after each power-on
- **AO** = voltage across the load resistor, which **rises with ammonia**. **DO** goes **LOW** when AO passes the trimmer threshold

## Pinout (module header, left to right as printed)
| Pin | Name | Function |
|-----|------|----------|
| 1 | VCC | 5 V supply (heater + comparator) |
| 2 | GND | Ground |
| 3 | DO | Digital output: HIGH (5 V via pull-up) in clean air, **LOW when NH3 is above the threshold** |
| 4 | AO | Analog output, 0 to about 5 V, rises with NH3 |

Some sellers' boards print the labels in another order. Go by the labels on your board.

## Electrical limits
- VCC: **4.9–5.1 V** (datasheet heater voltage VH 5.0 V ±0.1 V; standard test Vc 5.0 V ±0.1 V). The datasheet says over-voltage damages the heater. 3.3 V does not heat the sensor properly.
- Current: about 180 mA from 5 V (heater ≤ 900 mW). Budget for it on USB power.
- AO and DO are **5 V signals**. AO can exceed 3.3 V with a lot of gas, and DO sits at 5 V through the module's pull-up. ESP32 and Pico pins are 3.3 V max: put a divider (10 kΩ in series, 20 kΩ to GND gives ×2/3) on each.

## Interface details
Datasheet basic test circuit: Rs (sensor) and RL (load resistor) form a divider from Vc:
```
VRL (AO) = Vc × RL / (Rs + RL)
Rs = (Vc / VRL − 1) × RL
```
Concentration is read from **Rs/R0** on the datasheet's log-log curve (Fig. 3), where **R0 is the sensor's resistance in clean air**. The curve is typical only, so **calibrate R0 on your own sensor**. The driver gives the voltage, Rs and Rs/R0. It does not invent ppm.
RL on most modules is **1 kΩ (SMD marked 102)**; the datasheet's VRL curve (Fig. 5) uses 4.7 kΩ and many people fit 47 kΩ for more swing. Check yours and pass `rl=`.
DO comes from the LM393 comparing AO with the trimmer voltage. Turn the trimmer until the DO LED just goes out in clean air.

## Wiring
| Module | ESP32 | Raspberry Pi Pico | Raspberry Pi 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| VCC | 5V / VIN (USB 5 V) | VBUS (pin 40) | 5V (pin 2) |
| GND | GND | GND | GND |
| DO | any GPIO, e.g. GPIO25 (through divider) | any GP, e.g. GP2 (through divider) | any GPIO (through divider) |
| AO | GPIO32–39 (ADC1, through divider) | GP26/27/28 (ADC, through divider) | External ADC (MCP3008 / ADS1115) |

On the ESP32 use ADC1 pins (ADC2 does not work while Wi-Fi is on). The Raspberry Pi 4 has no ADC, so use DO alone or add one.

## Using the driver
```python
from mq137 import MQ137
import time
sensor = MQ137(32, 25, rl=1000, divider=1.0)  # AO GPIO, DO GPIO; divider=1.5 with a 10k/20k divider
time.sleep(120)                                # warm-up
sensor.calibrate()                             # R0 = Rs now, in clean air (datasheet definition)
while True:
    print('AO=%.2fV Rs=%.0f ohm Rs/R0=%.2f nh3=%s' % (sensor.voltage(), sensor.resistance(), sensor.ratio(), sensor.detected()))
    time.sleep(1)
```
`voltage()` averages 32 samples (`read_uv()` on ESP32, else `read_u16()` × 3.3 V). `detected()` is True when DO is LOW.

## Gotchas
- **Not a safety alarm.** Do not rely on this module to protect people from ammonia leaks.
- **DO is active LOW**: "nh3" = 0, "clear" = 1.
- **Hot and hungry**: the can gets hot, and about 180 mA can brown out a weak USB port. It is not a 3.3 V part.
- **Cross-sensitivity**: amines and other reducing gases also lower Rs.
- **Temperature and humidity** shift Rs (datasheet Fig. 4).
- **Silicone vapour, corrosive gas (H2S, SOx, Cl2, HCl), salt spray, water, condensation and freezing** permanently damage sensitivity (datasheet cautions).
