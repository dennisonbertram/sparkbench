# MQ-3 alcohol gas sensor module (MQ-3)

## Overview
The **MQ-3** (Hanwei) is a heated tin-dioxide (SnO2) gas sensor that is most sensitive to **alcohol (ethanol) vapour** and only weakly sensitive to benzine. Its resistance **Rs drops** as alcohol rises. Hobbyists buy it on a **4-pin module** (common blue FC-22-style board) that adds a load resistor, an **LM393 comparator**, a sensitivity trimmer, a power LED and a DO LED.
- Detects alcohol from **0.05 to 10 mg/L** (datasheet). Good for "more or less alcohol than before"; it is **not a calibrated breathalyser**
- Heater: **5 V ±0.1 V**, 33 Ω ±5 %, under 750 mW (about **150 mA**). It gets hot
- Sensing resistance Rs: **1–8 MΩ** at 0.4 mg/L alcohol; slope Rs(1 mg/L)/Rs(0.4 mg/L) ≤ 0.6
- Datasheet load resistor: **RL = 200 kΩ** (100–470 kΩ)
- Preheat: **over 24 hours** for a new sensor (datasheet), then a few minutes after each power-on
- **AO** = voltage across the load resistor, which **rises with alcohol**. **DO** goes **LOW** when AO passes the trimmer threshold

## Pinout (module header, sensor facing you, pins down, left to right)
| Pin | Name | Function |
|-----|------|----------|
| 1 | VCC | 5 V supply (heater + comparator) |
| 2 | GND | Ground |
| 3 | DO | Digital output: HIGH (5 V via pull-up) in clean air, **LOW when alcohol is above the threshold** |
| 4 | AO | Analog output, 0 to about 5 V, rises with alcohol |

## Electrical limits
- VCC: **4.9–5.1 V** (datasheet Vc and VH 5 V ±0.1 V). 3.3 V under-heats the sensor and readings are meaningless.
- Current: about 150 mA from 5 V. Budget for it on USB power.
- AO and DO are **5 V signals**. AO can exceed 3.3 V with strong alcohol, and DO sits at 5 V through the module's pull-up. ESP32 and Pico pins are 3.3 V max: use a divider (10 kΩ in series, 20 kΩ to GND gives ×2/3) on each.

## Interface details
Datasheet measuring circuit: Rs (sensor) and RL (load resistor) form a divider from Vc:
```
VRL (AO) = Vc × RL / (Rs + RL)
Rs = (Vc / VRL − 1) × RL
```
Alcohol level is read from **Rs/R0** on the datasheet's log-log chart (Fig. 2), where R0 is the sensor's resistance at **0.4 mg/L alcohol** (about 200 ppm) in clean air. The chart is typical only and Rs varies 1–8 MΩ between sensors, so **calibrate R0 on your own sensor**. The driver gives the voltage, Rs and Rs/R0. It does not invent mg/L.
Check the load resistor fitted on your module and pass `rl=` (the driver defaults to the datasheet's 200 kΩ). With a much smaller RL than the datasheet's, AO stays very low.
DO comes from the LM393 comparing AO with the trimmer voltage: turn the trimmer until the DO LED just goes out in clean air.

## Wiring
| Module | ESP32 | Raspberry Pi Pico | Raspberry Pi 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| VCC | 5V / VIN (USB 5 V) | VBUS (pin 40) | 5V (pin 2) |
| GND | GND | GND | GND |
| DO | any GPIO, e.g. GPIO25 (through divider) | any GP, e.g. GP2 (through divider) | any GPIO (through divider) |
| AO | GPIO32–39 (ADC1, through divider) | GP26/27/28 (ADC, through divider) | External ADC (MCP3008 / ADS1115) |

On the ESP32 use ADC1 pins (ADC2 does not work while Wi-Fi is on). The Raspberry Pi 4 has no ADC, so use DO alone or add one.

## Using the driver
```python
from mq3 import MQ3
import time
sensor = MQ3(32, 25, rl=200000, divider=1.0)  # AO GPIO, DO GPIO; divider=1.5 with a 10k/20k divider
time.sleep(120)                                # warm-up
sensor.calibrate()                             # R0 = Rs now (in your reference condition)
while True:
    print('AO=%.2fV Rs=%.0f ohm Rs/R0=%.2f alcohol=%s' % (sensor.voltage(), sensor.resistance(), sensor.ratio(), sensor.detected()))
    time.sleep(1)
```
`voltage()` averages 32 samples (`read_uv()` on ESP32, else `read_u16()` × 3.3 V). `detected()` is True when DO is LOW.

## Gotchas
- **DO is active LOW**: "alcohol" = 0, "clear" = 1. Some tutorials have this backwards.
- **Hot and hungry**: the can gets hot, and 150 mA can brown out a weak USB port. It is not a 3.3 V part.
- **Warm-up drift**: readings creep for minutes after power-on and for a day on a new sensor.
- **Temperature and humidity** shift Rs/R0 (datasheet Fig. 4): set alarm points with that in mind.
- **Not a legal breathalyser**: do not use it to decide whether someone may drive.
- Also responds to benzine/petrol vapour, hexane and, weakly, LPG and CO (datasheet Fig. 2).
